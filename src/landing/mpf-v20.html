Why AIWhat AI changes for an investor, and where it fails

Ask AI whether your fund is a good one, and it tends to say yes. Ask it the right way, with the right data, and it does hours of research in minutes.

The limit is the LLM itself (the large language model behind tools such as ChatGPT): it tends to agree with the view in your question, which researchers call sycophancy, so the question you ask and the data you give it determine the answer. Used the right way, it changes three things for an investor, because AI companies now sell intelligence by the token: a fund's monthly portfolio or ten years of a company's annual reports can be summarised in minutes, so checking a fund before you invest no longer takes an evening; your allocation can be rebuilt whenever your income, goals or the market change; and any decision can be checked against the strongest argument against it before you act. Asking the right way needs basics most people were never taught. So this workshop teaches those basics first, mutual funds in most depth, then stocks, fixed income, commodities and insurance, and only then shows the questions I ask a model so that it does not simply agree with me.

Why returns alone are not enoughThe first check the workshop teaches

Learn to check a mutual fund's risk, not only its past returns.

Many fund lists rank funds only by past returns. Fund A and Fund B both returned 13% a year for 10 years, so such a list shows them as equals.

They were not equal. At the worst point, Fund B fell 41%; Fund A fell 28%. On ₹1,00,000 that is ₹59,000 against ₹72,000, and Fund B took 17 months to recover, 10 months longer than Fund A.

A model given only the returns list cannot report the fall: the fall is not in its data. Give it the NAV history (each fund's daily prices) and the published portfolios, and it reports the 41% fall. That is the first check on Day 1, by hand and then with a model.

Second check: the fund against its benchmark index (the market index it is measured against). Over the 10 years to December 2025, 76% of actively managed large-cap funds in India returned less than their benchmark, the S&P India LargeMidCap.

Source: S&P Dow Jones Indices, SPIVA India Scorecard, Year-End 2025.

Who teaches you

Nikhil Sharma · Delhi, India · Former risk analyst, Goldman Sachs

I started my career at Goldman Sachs stress-testing hedge funds and mutual funds: working out how much each fund would lose in a severe market fall.

My job was to find the market conditions in which each fund could lose the most money. Over my time there, I analysed the risk of $65 billion in mutual fund and hedge-fund assets. I saw how a portfolio is built, both top-down (from the economy to the sector to the stock) and bottom-up (from the company to the stock): what gets a stock into a portfolio and what keeps it out, what a fund manager cares about, and what limits them. Some of those limits are set by regulators and some by the fund industry itself.

So I did not only learn about mutual funds. I also learned fixed income (bonds), equity (shares), commodities, foreign exchange and options (contracts whose possible gains and losses are not symmetrical).

If I had not measured fund risk by hand for years before AI, I would not know which of a model's answers to trust, which to check and which to ignore. In this workshop I teach the basics of personal finance and, for each topic, show how I use AI without writing any code: to explain what I had not understood, to go into more detail where I wanted it, and to do the analysis I did not have the time or the data to do myself.

Level 1 · Beginner · Day 1

How mutual funds work

What you own when you buy a mutual fund, which 3 to 5 of SEBI's (the market regulator's) roughly 37 fund categories most investors need, and how a fixed deposit compares with a debt fund (a fund that holds bonds instead of shares).

With AI: a fund's factsheet (the fund house's monthly one-page summary) explained in plain words, term by term, by a model instructed to use only the text of that document.

You walk away with a one-page summary of the fund categories you need.

Level 2 · Beginner · Day 1

The costs investors often miss

How the expense ratio (the yearly fee) and exit loads (a charge for selling early) reduce your return every year, how capital gains tax reduces it when you sell, and what a SIP, a lump sum and a step-up SIP (a SIP that rises each year) each cost you over ten years.

With AI: the ten-year cost of your own SIP worked out and explained, with the line in the fund's Scheme Information Document (its official rulebook) that each figure comes from.

You walk away with a checklist of the costs you pay on each fund.

Level 3 · Intermediate · Day 1

Check any fund in 5 minutes

How far a fund fell in past market declines (maximum drawdown), its rolling returns (returns measured from every possible start date, not one), and how its return compares with its benchmark index.

With AI: a fund's monthly portfolio disclosure, read and compared in minutes instead of an evening, with the model asked a leading question (one that suggests its own answer) and then a neutral one.

You walk away with a 5-minute check you can run on any fund.

Level 4 · Intermediate · Day 2

Overlap and your asset allocation

How to measure how many stocks two funds have in common, and how to work out your asset allocation from your income, expenses, EMIs (loan instalments) and goals.

With AI: you give a model your own numbers and a set of rules, and it returns an allocation with the reasoning for each line, which you then check and change.

You walk away with an asset allocation you have worked out yourself.

Level 5 · Advanced · Day 2

Stocks: the basics

How an analyst studies a company before buying: revenue, profit margins (profit as a share of revenue), debt, cash flow and valuation (the price paid for each rupee of profit). Then how to judge a stock idea, wherever it comes from, before acting on it.

With AI: ten years of a company's annual reports summarised into a one-page note, and the model asked to list the reasons against the investment before the reasons for it.

You walk away with a fixed list of steps for studying any company.

Level 6 · Advanced · Day 2

When the market falls, and the basics of gold, deposits and insurance

What happened in past declines such as March 2020, how long the market took to recover, and how to write a plan for the next one before it happens. In 15 minutes, the basics in India of gold, fixed deposits, term insurance (life cover for a fixed number of years) and health insurance: what is insurance and what is an investment sold as insurance.

With AI: your plan tested against past market declines using the same method a risk analyst uses on a fund, and your retirement withdrawals tested against every starting year since 1980.

You walk away with a written plan for a 30% market fall.

How I think about advice

Nobodyhasacrystalball.

Takeownershipofyourowndecisions.

PhilosophyReasoningPredictionEmotional temperament

None of the six levels ends with a fund or a stock to buy. I have never given investment advice to others, and I think it is a bad habit to have. Nobody has a crystal ball, whether a fund manager or an adviser. The key to being a good investor is taking ownership: your decisions are yours, not attributed to something somebody else said. If you choose stocks, you pick the companies. If you choose mutual funds, you pick the funds. If you decide to keep all of it in cash, I will not fight you. It is your money.

"But I do not know as much." That is a cop-out. Investing is not rocket science: it is adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing. There is nothing I do in investing that is too deep or too profound for you to do yourself with some time and some practice. What I give you is my reasoning for my own choices, and a mechanism (a fixed set of checks) you can use to turn your reasoning into your choices. The workshop is about how you think, how you reason, how you make predictions and how you control your own temperament. AI is useful only after you have done that thinking.

Before you book.

Is this investment advice? Will you tell me what to buy?

No. This is an educational workshop. You learn how to evaluate an allocation, a fund, a stock or a bond, but nothing is recommended. For personal investment advice, please consult a SEBI-registered investment adviser.

When exactly is the workshop?

Day 1 is Saturday {class date}; Day 2 is Sunday {day 2 date}. Both days are live online. The start time is shown above and in the session link, which is sent 24 hours before Day 1.

Do I need to know how to code, or pay for an AI tool?

No. No code is written in the workshop. Everything is done in the chat window of a model. The free versions of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or DeepSeek are enough to do every exercise in the workshop. A paid plan mainly raises the number of messages and file uploads allowed; the workshop is designed to be followed on a free tier.

Which AI tool do you use, and does it matter?

I use more than one and show you how to ask the same question of two models and compare. The method matters more than the tool: the question you ask and the data you attach determine the quality of the answer, and both are taught on Day 1.

Is this suitable for a first-time investor?

Yes. Level 1 starts with how a mutual fund works and builds from there, step by step, in simple language, using real funds. Every term is explained when it is first used.

I already invest. Will this be too basic?

Probably not. Many investors have not checked their funds' maximum drawdown, overlap or return against the benchmark, or what a model says when it is given the published data instead of a leading question. On Day 2, these checks are run live.

Is this stock tips?

No. Level 5 teaches how an analyst studies a company and where to find companies worth studying. No company is recommended to buy, sell or hold.

Will you check my own portfolio?

No. The checks are shown on a sample portfolio of seven real funds. You can run the same checks on your own holdings privately, using the method and the free tools in guide 4.

Do I need to attend both days?

Yes. Day 1 covers how funds work, what they cost and how to measure their risk. Day 2 runs those checks live, then covers your allocation, stocks, and the basics of deposits, gold and insurance. Each day builds on the one before.

Is it live, or a recording?

Live. You can ask questions in the chat at any time, and they are answered during the session.

They are shared during the live workshop. They are yours to keep.
